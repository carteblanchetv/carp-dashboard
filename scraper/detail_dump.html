The Loneliness Club

By: Latashia Naidoo | Commission

Proposal


Show: Carte Blanche

One Liner (Tonight on Carte Blanche...):
In a small house in Grassy Park, pensioners gather each day not just for care - but for companionship in a world growing lonelier by the year.

Unique Selling Point:
In Cape Town, a modest home is becoming an emotional lifeline for elderly people navigating the quiet epidemic of loneliness.
It's a daycare - for the elderly.

Summary:
The Loneliness Club:

Every morning, cars begin arriving outside this modest house in Cape Town’s Grassy Park. Drop-off starts around 7:15am.
People are helped carefully out of passenger seats. Bags are unpacked. Jerseys are folded over chairs. There’s tea already brewing in the kitchen. Inside, the day begins with music.
There are games and laughter and little exercise classes. Painting, singalongs, dancing.
And then after lunch, the mood changes completely. The lights dim.
Blankets come out. The room goes quiet.
It’s nap time.
One by one, everybody settles down for an afternoon sleep while they wait to be fetched at three o’clock.
And honestly, if you watched it from a distance, it would look almost exactly like a daycare centre.
Except the people sleeping under those blankets are in their sixties, seventies and eighties.

And that’s what makes this story so emotionally powerful.
Because inside this ordinary home in Grassy Park, 53-year-old Estherline Meyer has quietly created something extraordinary: an elderly daycare centre called Leap of Faith for families who can no longer safely leave ageing parents alone during the day while they work.
It operates Monday to Friday, from 8am to 3pm, with cognitive exercises, games, music, gentle physical activity, meals, companionship, and structure.
But emotionally, what’s really happening here is much deeper. This is a story about loneliness.
Quiet loneliness.
The kind that settles slowly into old age after spouses die, children emigrate, bodies slow down, memories fade, and entire days pass without meaningful conversation.
Some of the pensioners who come here are widowed. Some spend most of the week alone. Some are beginning to struggle cognitively. Others simply don’t want to sit in silence all day waiting for somebody to come home.
And what makes the story nuanced is that their families aren’t failing them.
They’re exhausted.
Adult children are trying to survive modern life while carrying ageing parents at the same time - balancing work, finances, caregiving, school runs, and enormous guilt about leaving loved ones alone all day.
So this house becomes something far bigger than daycare. It becomes an emotional safety net.
A place where somebody notices if your mother hasn’t eaten lunch. Or taken her medication. Or laughed that day.

And globally, the story taps into this fascinating and deeply unsettling conversation around human connection.
Researchers are increasingly linking chronic loneliness to depression, dementia, cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, and premature death. Around the world, people are beginning to create entire economies around companionship: renting friends in Japan, paying for cuddling services in South Africa, searching for ways to fill emotional absences modern life keeps creating.
But inside this little house in Grassy Park, the antidote is incredibly simple: music, routine, conversation, touch, tea, community… and nap time.

Visually, it’s rich with warmth and texture: morning drop-offs that feel like a school run in reverse, pensioners dancing in slippers, old friends teasing each other over a fame of dominoes, blankets lined up across the lounge, someone standing quietly at the gate waiting to be fetched.
It’s funny, heartbreaking, tender, and deeply human. And underneath all of it is this really haunting question:
What happens when people are living longer than ever before… but feeling increasingly alone?
